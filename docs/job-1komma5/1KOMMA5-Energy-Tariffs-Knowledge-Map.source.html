1.3 The product that explains the whole strategy: "free electricity"

In Sweden, 1KOMMA5° launched Dynamic Pulse: customers whose PV and battery are connected to Heartbeat get electricity at 0 cent/kWh for 15,000 kWh per year. You should be able to explain why this is not a giveaway:

  • The customer surrenders control of the battery (and increasingly the heat pump / EV) to the aggregator.
  • The aggregator monetises that control: spot arbitrage (charge cheap / discharge expensive), intraday trading, balancing-market capacity, avoided grid fees, and imbalance-portfolio smoothing.
  • If the flexibility value captured per customer per year exceeds the wholesale cost of that customer's energy, giving the energy away is rational — and it is an extremely strong acquisition and retention weapon.
  • It also locks the customer to the platform: leaving means losing free electricity. Churn drops.

The follow-up question you should be ready for: "would this work in Germany?" A defensible answer names the blockers rather than guessing: German retail price is ~two-thirds non-commodity (grid fees, levies, taxes, VAT) which you cannot zero out; battery-only flexibility earns less where spot spreads are narrower; and §14a curtailment plus metering coverage limit how much flexibility is actually addressable. So the German version is more likely to be a deep discount on the commodity component than a headline zero.

TR: İsveç'teki "bedava elektrik" ürünü hediye değil: müşteri bataryanın kontrolünü veriyor, şirket bu esnekliği piyasada paraya çeviriyor. 2.2 Wholesale markets — where the price in your tariff comes from

A dynamic tariff is a retail wrapper around a wholesale price. You must know the sequence:

  1. Forward / futures market (EEX) — years, quarters, months ahead. Used for hedging fixed-price tariffs.
  2. Day-ahead auction (EPEX SPOT) — the reference price for almost every dynamic tariff. Bids close at 12:00 CET for the following day; results are published early afternoon. Since 1 October 2025 the German day-ahead auction clears in 15-minute market time units (MTU) — 96 prices per day instead of 24. It is a uniform-price (single clearing price) auction, coupled across Europe via SDAC.
  3. Intraday — continuous trading up to shortly before delivery, plus intraday auctions (IDA). This is where forecast errors get corrected. Coupled via SIDC.
  4. Balancing / Regelleistung — TSO-procured reserves: FCR (Primärregelleistung), aFRR (Sekundärregelleistung), mFRR (Minutenreserve). Aggregated batteries can prequalify and earn capacity + energy payments here.
  5. Redispatch — TSO-instructed generation shifts to relieve grid congestion. Redispatch 2.0 obligations apply from 100 kW upward, so single households are out of scope, but pooled assets are strategically relevant.
The 15-minute switch is a product event, not a technicality. 96 MTUs/day means: sharper price peaks and troughs, more arbitrage value for a battery, better matching of PV shape, more data volume (35,040 intervals/year per meter), and billing logic that must be quarter-hour native. Any tariff product built on hourly assumptions is now structurally mispriced. Expect this to come up.
TR: Sıra şu: vadeli piyasa (EEX) → gün-öncesi ihale (EPEX, saat 12:00 kapanış) → gün-içi (intraday) → dengeleme (FCR/aFRR/mFRR) → redispatch. 1 Ekim 2025'ten beri Alman gün-öncesi piyasası 15 dakikalık dilimlerle işliyor: günde 96 fiyat. Bu, batarya için daha çok arbitraj fırsatı ve faturalama mantığında çeyrek-saat zorunluluğu demek. Ürün açısından kritik bir değişiklik.

Price formation you should be able to explain in 30 seconds

Merit order: plants are dispatched cheapest-marginal-cost first (renewables ≈ 0 → nuclear/lignite → coal → gas → oil). The last plant needed sets the price for everyone. So gas often sets the price even when renewables dominate volume. When renewable supply exceeds inflexible demand, prices go negative — producers pay to feed in. Negative prices are the single most important phenomenon for your product: they are simultaneously the reason dynamic tariffs are attractive, the reason feed-in support is being withdrawn, and the reason flexibility has value.

TR: Merit order = en ucuz santral önce devreye girer, fiyatı son devreye giren santral belirler. Bu yüzden yenilenebilir çok olsa bile fiyatı sık sık gaz belirler. Arz talebi aştığında fiyat negatife düşer. Negatif fiyatlar bu işin merkezinde: dinamik tarifin cazibesi, EEG desteğinin geri çekilmesi ve esnekliğin değeri hep buradan geliyor.

2.4 Dynamic tariffs as a product

Legal base: since 1 January 2025, §41a EnWG obliges every German electricity supplier to offer a dynamic tariff. This turned a differentiator into a commodity feature — so competition moved to optimisation quality, hardware integration and transparency, not to the existence of the product.

Price build-up of a dynamic tariff

Customer price per 15 min = Day-ahead price (MTU) + supplier markup (ct/kWh) + grid fee + levies + taxes + VAT, plus a monthly base fee (Grundgebühr).

Business modelMechanicsTrade-off
Pure passthrough0 markup on spot; revenue purely from the base fee (Ostrom-style)Maximum trust, zero margin flexibility, no buffer for imbalance cost
Markup + low base feeSmall ct/kWh markup, minimal monthly fee (aWATTar-style)Margin scales with volume; attractive to low-consumption customers
Ecosystem-bundledTariff attached to hardware + HEMS; margin comes from flexibility and the bundle, not the ct/kWh (1KOMMA5°, Tibber)Higher headline price, higher total customer value; requires the hardware to be there
Flex-for-energy swapVery low or zero commodity price in exchange for asset control (Dynamic Pulse)Best retention weapon; only works where flex value > energy cost

Risks that sit behind the price — know these names

  • Volume risk — the customer consumes more/less than forecast.
  • Profile / shape risk — when they consume differs from the hedged shape. A dynamic tariff transfers most of this to the customer, which is precisely why it can be cheaper.
  • Imbalance risk (Ausgleichsenergie) — deviation between the schedule and reality, settled at the imbalance price. Owned by the BKV.
  • Basis risk — the hedge and the actual delivery point/period do not match (very relevant after the 15-min switch).
  • Credit / bad debt risk — rises when prices spike.
  • Regulatory risk — a levy or ruling changes the cost stack mid-contract.
TR: §41a EnWG uyarınca 2025'ten beri Almanya'da her tedarikçi dinamik tarif sunmak zorunda — yani ürünün varlığı artık fark değil, kalitesi fark. Fiyat kurgusu: spot fiyat + tedarikçi marjı + şebeke + vergi + KDV, artı aylık sabit ücret. Arkasındaki riskler: hacim, profil/şekil, dengeleme (Ausgleichsenergie), baz riski, tahsilat, mevzuat. Dinamik tarifin ucuz olmasının sebebi, profil riskinin müşteriye devredilmesi.

2.5 Metering — the physical precondition for everything

  • MsbG (Messstellenbetriebsgesetz) governs metering. iMSys = intelligentes Messsystem: a digital meter plus a Smart Meter Gateway (SMGW) with certified communication. A moderne Messeinrichtung (mME) is digital but not communicating — it cannot support a dynamic tariff on its own.
  • 15-minute interval values from the iMSys are what makes dynamic billing, energy sharing and §14a Module 3 possible. No iMSys, no real dynamic product.
  • The CLS channel in the SMGW is the standardised path for controlling devices behind the meter — the regulated alternative to a vendor cloud.
  • Since the Solarspitzengesetz (in force 25 February 2025): new PV systems from 7 kW must be equipped with an iMSys plus a control box by the metering point operator. Without an intelligent metering system, feed-in is capped at 60% of nominal capacity (§9(2) EEG 2023).
  • Workaround used by the market: optical meter readers (e.g. Tibber Pulse-type devices) read a modern meter locally and stream consumption to the app. Good enough for transparency and optimisation; not a substitute for regulated billing data.
TR: iMSys = akıllı sayaç + iletişim kutusu (SMGW). mME = dijital ama iletişimsiz sayaç — dinamik tarif için yetmez. 15 dakikalık veri olmadan dinamik faturalama, energy sharing ve §14a Modül 3 mümkün değil. Solarspitzengesetz'ten (25 Şubat 2025) beri 7 kW üstü yeni PV'lerde iMSys + kontrol kutusu zorunlu; yoksa besleme %60 ile sınırlanıyor. Optik okuyucular (Tibber Pulse tipi) şeffaflık sağlar ama resmi faturalama verisi yerine geçmez.

2.6 Flexibility and the virtual power plant

A VPP (virtuelles Kraftwerk) pools many small assets so they behave, commercially and technically, like one dispatchable plant. Heartbeat is 1KOMMA5°'s VPP: PV, home batteries, heat pumps, EV chargers and wallboxes across >100,000 systems, now ~1 GW of shiftable load.

Physical numbers to hold in your head (defensible ranges, not exact claims)

  • Residential PV system: 8–12 kWp; German specific yield ≈ 950–1,100 kWh per kWp per year.
  • Home battery: 5–15 kWh usable; C-rate ~0.5–1; round-trip efficiency 85–92%; ~6,000–10,000 cycles for LFP; usable depth of discharge 90–100% for LFP.
  • Heat pump: COP 3–4.5 at design point; annual SCOP / JAZ 3.0–4.2 in German retrofits — the seasonal figure is the honest one.
  • EV: 40–80 kWh battery, home AC charging 11 kW (3-phase); a single EV roughly doubles household consumption.
  • Average German household: ~2,500–4,000 kWh/year; with heat pump + EV, 8,000–15,000 kWh/year.
  • §14a curtailment floor: a controllable device may be dimmed but at least 4.2 kW must remain available.

Always present these as ranges with the assumption stated. In energy, a confident single number that is wrong destroys credibility faster than an honest range.

2.7 Direktvermarktung — the regulatory launch named in the job description

Three ways a PV system's output can be monetised in Germany:

ModelHow it worksWho it suits

(fixed feed-in tariff)		DSO pays a fixed statutory rate per kWh exported. Simple, no market exposure.Small rooftops that want zero complexity

Why this is suddenly a product opportunity for small rooftops

  • The Solarspitzengesetz substantially simplified direct marketing below 100 kWp — the administrative barrier that used to make it uneconomic for a 10 kWp roof has come down.
  • At the same time, §51 EEG logic was extended: once an iMSys is installed, no feed-in remuneration is paid for hours when the exchange price is negative. Fixed feed-in is therefore getting structurally worse.
  • Result: for a household with iMSys + battery + HEMS, "sell my surplus intelligently" starts to beat "collect a shrinking fixed rate". That is a new product line, and it is what the JD means by "regulatory product launches (e.g. Direktvermarktung)".

What the Direktvermarkter actually has to do — the operational reality

  1. Forecast generation per 15-minute interval (weather models + system-specific model).
  2. Bid into day-ahead by 12:00 CET; correct on intraday as forecasts update.
  3. Register the asset with the grid operator and assign it to a Bilanzkreis.
  4. Submit schedules (Fahrpläne) to the TSO — reported in defined increments.
  5. Carry the imbalance risk: every forecast error is settled as Ausgleichsenergie at the imbalance price, which can be brutal in tight hours.
  6. Settle and invoice the plant owner, and claim the market premium.
The product question you should be able to frame — and you would be unusually strong if you framed it unprompted:
"For a 10 kWp rooftop with a battery, at what monthly average market value does Direktvermarktung beat the fixed feed-in tariff, once you subtract the direct marketer's fee, the imbalance cost from forecast error, and the hours where negative prices zero out the alternative anyway? And do we pass the imbalance risk to the customer or absorb it in the portfolio?"

That single question demonstrates you understand the economics, the operations and the customer-experience trade-off at once. Aggregating thousands of small rooftops is exactly how a company like 1KOMMA5° makes that maths work: portfolio effects cancel individual forecast errors.

2.8 §14a EnWG — grid-side control and time-variable grid fees

  • Since 1 January 2024, DSOs must connect new controllable consumption devices — heat pumps, wallboxes, home batteries — and in exchange may curtail them (down to a 4.2 kW floor) when the local grid is stressed. Connection can no longer be refused for grid reasons.
  • In return, the customer chooses one of three modules:
    • Module 1 — flat annual grid-fee discount, roughly €110–190/year. No second meter, simplest billing.
    • Module 2 — percentage reduction on the grid-fee energy component; requires a separate meter; worthwhile for heavy users.
    • Module 3 — time-variable grid fees (available since April 2025): three price windows per day; requires a Smart Meter Gateway. Combinable with Module 1.
  • From mid-2026 a revised BNetzA determination applies with stricter requirements, and grid-oriented control shifts from a voluntary option to a precondition for the DSO to connect controllable loads promptly.
Why the tariff team cares: Module 3 + a dynamic tariff creates a two-signal optimisation problem — the HEMS must minimise (spot price + time-variable grid fee) jointly, and those two signals do not always point the same way. Cheap spot hours can coincide with expensive grid windows. Solving that well is a genuine, currently unsolved product differentiator, and it is a first-class thing to raise in an interview.

2.9 Energy sharing — a brand-new product surface (live 1 June 2026)

  • §42c EnWG made energy sharing legally possible in Germany from 1 June 2026: sharing self-generated renewable electricity with other people or companies through the public distribution grid.
  • Difference from existing models: Mieterstrom and gemeinschaftliche Gebäudeversorgung (GGV) stay inside one building; energy sharing crosses buildings — streets, blocks, neighbourhoods.
  • Scope: initially within the same DSO settlement area; from June 2028 extendable to neighbouring grid areas within the same control zone.
  • Requirements: iMSys on both sides, a joint-use agreement, and each participant still needs a separate supply contract for residual power.
  • Reality check: all DSOs are legally obliged to enable it from June 2026, but several have already signalled that technical implementation will come later. Expect a messy rollout — which for a well-organised supplier is an advantage, not a problem.

This is only weeks old as a live regime. If you can describe it accurately, you are current with the market — and "what should our energy-sharing product look like, and which DSO areas are actually ready?" is a perfect intern initiative.

2.10 Marktkommunikation — the section that will set you apart

The JD says: "Dive into our market communication workflows, identify inefficiencies, and drive initiatives that make our energy retail engine faster and more reliable." Here is what that sentence actually means.

What Marktkommunikation (MaKo) is

Standardised, machine-to-machine business-document exchange between all market partners in the German energy sector — supplier, DSO, metering operator, balance responsible party. It uses EDIFACT (the UN standard, in a BDEW-defined energy subset), exchanged over defined secure transport. Every customer relationship in German energy retail is created, changed and ended by these messages. When they fail, the customer is not supplied, not billed, or billed wrongly.

The rulebooks

RulebookFull nameCovers
GPKEGeschäftsprozesse zur Kundenbelieferung mit ElektrizitätSupplier switching, move-in / move-out, master-data maintenance — the core retail processes
WiMWechselprozesse im MesswesenChanging the metering point operator, meter operations
MaBiSMarktregeln für die Bilanzkreisabrechnung StromBalance-group settlement: aggregating volumes and settling them between market participants
GeLi GasGeschäftsprozesse Lieferantenwechsel GasThe gas equivalent of GPKE

The identifiers — get these right or nothing works

  • MaLo-ID (Marktlokations-ID) — an 11-digit number identifying a market location: a point where energy is consumed or fed in. This is the central identifier of the German electricity and gas market; supplier switches, balance-group assignment, meter-value transmission and billing all key off it.
  • MeLo-ID (Messlokations-ID) — identifies the metering location (the physical meter). One market location can relate to several metering locations, and vice versa. Confusing the two is a classic, expensive error.
  • Bilanzkreis + EIC codes — the balancing group the location is assigned to, and the standard market-participant codes.

The processes and the messages

ProcessTypical message types
Lieferantenwechsel (supplier switch)UTILMD (master data / registration), acknowledged by CONTRL / APERAK
Einzug / Auszug (move in / move out)UTILMD

Invoicing between market partnersINVOIC, remittance advice REMADV

Why it breaks — and therefore what "improving MaKo" means

Failure modes, in rough order of frequency:

  • Wrong or missing MaLo-ID, or a MaLo/MeLo mix-up → registration rejected.
  • Mismatched master data (name, address, meter number) between supplier and DSO → rejection.
  • Deadlines missed: GPKE processes have statutory windows; a late message restarts the process and pushes the supply start date.
  • Format/version drift after a BNetzA rulebook update (these change on fixed dates and everyone has to migrate).
  • Silent failures — a rejection nobody picked up, so the customer sits in limbo for weeks.
  • Each of those lands as a manual clearing case, a support ticket, a delayed first invoice, and eventually churn.
How to turn this into a credible intern initiative — this is your best single answer to "what would you work on?":
  1. Instrument the funnel: contract signed → UTILMD sent → acknowledged → supply start → first invoice. Measure conversion and elapsed time at each step.
  2. Classify every rejection by message type × error code × DSO. Almost always a small number of causes produce most of the volume.
  3. Quantify the cost: manual minutes per case × case volume, plus delayed-revenue days, plus first-90-day churn attributable to a failed start.
  4. Fix the top causes at the source — validation at the point of data capture beats clearing downstream. Propose which checks move into the signup flow.
  5. Report one number to the business: switch success rate and median time-to-supply, by market and by DSO.

3. Product management craft in a regulated energy business — 25%

3.1 What "own a well-scoped initiative" means in practice

Six steps. Interns who skip steps 1 and 6 are the ones who do not get the full-time offer.

  1. Frame the problem as a number. Not "our switching is slow" but "median time-to-supply is 34 days versus a 21-day target; each extra day costs X in delayed revenue and Y in churn risk."
  2. Get the data yourself. Do not wait for a dashboard. Pull it, clean it, sanity-check the total against a known figure.
  3. Produce 2–3 real options with cost, effort, risk and a recommendation. One option must always be "do nothing" with its cost stated.
  4. Pressure-test with the people who own the consequences — trading for price risk, legal for compliance, ops for workload, engineering for feasibility.
  5. Implement in the smallest shippable slice. One market, one segment, one DSO.
  6. Measure and publish the result, including when it did not work. Reporting a failed experiment honestly buys more trust than a vague success.

4. Data & tooling — 15%, but where you already have an edge

The JD asks for "analytical and structured thinker, comfortable with data and spreadsheets (Google Sheets a must, SQL or Python a plus)". So data is a supporting skill here — but it is your supporting skill, and it is how you will produce work faster than the other interns.

4.1 Google Sheets, to a professional standard

  • QUERY() — SQL-like filtering and aggregation inside Sheets. The single highest-leverage function; learn it properly.
  • SUMIFS / COUNTIFS / AVERAGEIFS, XLOOKUP (or INDEX/MATCH), ARRAYFORMULA, UNIQUE, FILTER, SPLIT.
  • Pivot tables with calculated fields; two-way data tables for sensitivity; named ranges; data validation; conditional formatting for threshold flags.
  • IMPORTDATA / IMPORTJSON-style pulls for price feeds if a live sheet is needed.
  • Model hygiene: inputs / calculations / outputs on separate blocks or tabs; units in labels; a "read me" cell; no magic numbers.

4.2 SQL you will actually use

  • SELECT / WHERE / GROUP BY / HAVING / ORDER BY; all four join types and when an outer join hides a data-quality bug.
  • Window functions — the ones that matter for interval energy data: LAG() / LEAD() for deltas between consecutive intervals, SUM() OVER (PARTITION BY ... ORDER BY ...) for cumulative volume, ROW_NUMBER() for deduplication, AVG() OVER for rolling baselines.
  • DATE_TRUNC / bucketing to 15-minute, hourly, daily granularity; calendar joins so missing intervals show as gaps instead of vanishing.
  • CTEs (WITH) to keep multi-step logic readable — this is a communication skill as much as a technical one.
  • A cohort/retention query: customers grouped by signup month, activity by month since signup.

4.3 Time-series realities that cause real bugs

  • 96 intervals per day, 35,040 per year at 15-minute resolution. Per meter. Volume grows fast.
  • Daylight saving time: in the CET/CEST zone one day per year has 23 hours and one has 25. Every naive × 96 assumption breaks twice a year. Store and compute in UTC, display in local time.
  • Missing and substitute values (Ersatzwerte): meter data arrives late, incomplete, or estimated. Never silently treat missing as zero — a missing interval and a zero-consumption interval mean completely different things commercially.
  • kW vs kWh: power vs energy. At 15-minute resolution, energy = average power × 0.25 h. Mixing these up is the most common analytical error in the industry.
  • Sign conventions: import positive / export negative, or two separate registers? Get this defined before you compute anything.
  • Restatements: meter values get corrected after the fact, so yesterday's report legitimately changes. Version your numbers.

8. Honest risk register

RiskSeverityHow to handle it
German languageHighThe JD asks for excellent German. Do not bluff. State your real level, note that you can read regulatory German (EnWG/EEG/BNetzA terminology — true after Section 7), commit to a concrete improvement path, and point out that MaKo and price analysis are largely structured-data work. Ask directly what the day-to-day language of the team is — some 1KOMMA5° global product teams operate in English.
Work permit / ZusatzblattHighThey asked in writing. Have scans ready before the call and know your permitted hours. This is administrative, not a judgement on you — but arriving unprepared reads as carelessness.
6 months, ideally full-timeMediumCheck this against your MSc obligations and your freelance/EnergyLens commitments before the call, and give a clear answer. Ambiguity here worries hiring managers more than a "no".
Profile fit vs. business/econ studentsMediumYou will be compared against candidates with consulting internships. Your counter is not "I also know business" — it is that you can build the analysis they can only request. Lead with the tariff backtest and the unit-economics model.
Over-claiming on EnergyLensMediumSynthetic data, no completed pilot. Say it first, every time.
Being seen as "the data person"Low-MediumThis is a product role. Whenever you describe a technical capability, close the loop with the decision it enabled — otherwise you get filed as an analyst, not a future PM.
TR: Gerçek riskler sıralı: (1) Almanca — bluff yapma, gerçek seviyeyi + mevzuat Almancasını okuyabildiğini + somut planı söyle ve ekibin günlük dilini sor (1KOMMA5°'nin global ürün ekipleri sıklıkla İngilizce çalışıyor). (2) Çalışma izni — belgeleri görüşmeden önce hazırla, izinli saatini bil. (3) 6 ay tam zamanlı — yüksek lisans ve freelance yükünle çakışmasını önceden netleştir; belirsizlik "hayır"dan daha çok endişe yaratır. (4) Profil karşılaştırması — danışmanlık stajı yapmış işletme öğrencileriyle yarışıyorsun; kozun "ben de iş bilirim" değil, "onların sadece talep edebildiği analizi ben kurabiliyorum". (5) EnergyLens'te abartma. (6) "Veri adamı" damgası yememek için her teknik yetenek anlatımını, sağladığı karar ile kapat.

Closing note

Confidence in this domain does not come from knowing everything — the German energy market is genuinely too large for that, and everyone in the room knows it. It comes from three things: knowing the structure (who does what, in what order, under which rule), knowing your numbers (and their sources and their sensitivity), and being precise about what you do not know. You already have the hardest part, which is the ability to build. This document is the vocabulary and the map to put around it.

TR: Bu alanda güven her şeyi bilmekten gelmez — Alman enerji piyasası bunun için fazla geniş ve odadaki herkes bunu biliyor. Güven üç şeyden gelir: yapıyı bilmek (kim ne yapıyor, hangi sırada, hangi kurala göre), sayılarını bilmek (kaynağı ve duyarlılığıyla) ve bilmediğin şeyi net söylemek.
